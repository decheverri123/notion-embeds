Theory Lesson (about 20 minutes)

Quick recall

Last week, treble clef lines were E G B D F and spaces spelled F A C E. You drilled naming notes cold and started to feel the shapes rather than count them. Hold onto that habit, because you are about to build the same habit in a new neighborhood.

Meet the bass clef

The bass clef (also called the F clef) is the low staff on the piano and the home of the bass guitar, cello, trombone, and your left hand. The two dots of the symbol wrap around the F line, so the second line from the top is always F. That is the anchor. Everything else grows out of it.

Reading from the bottom line up, the lines are G, B, D, F, A. A friendly mnemonic: "Good Boys Do Fine Always." The spaces, again from the bottom, are A, C, E, G, which spells "All Cows Eat Grass." Middle C lives on the first ledger line above the bass staff, and also on the first ledger line below the treble staff. Same note, two homes. That shared note is the hinge of the grand staff.

Worked example on piano

Sit at the keyboard, find middle C with your right thumb, then slide your left thumb one octave lower to the C below. Play these bass clef notes one at a time and say the name out loud as you play: C, E, G, B, D. You are walking up the bottom of a C major scale plus a couple of extras. Notice how the low G you played is exactly the bottom line of the bass staff.

Worked example on guitar

On guitar we usually read treble clef, but every bass clef note maps directly to the low strings. The open 6th string (low E) sits on the bottom space of the bass clef. The open 5th string (A) is the top space. The open 4th string (D) is the middle line. So when you see a bass clef in a piano score, you can find those pitches on your bottom three strings without a capo. Try playing E, A, D open, then fret G on the low E string (3rd fret) and C on the A string (3rd fret). You have just voiced the first half of "All Cows Eat Grass" in real time.

Sheet music examples

Example 1 walks up the lines of the bass clef (G, B, D, F, A) and then walks up the spaces (A, C, E, G). Play the recording, then read it silently and say each name out loud before checking yourself with the audio.

Example 2 climbs from the bottom of the bass staff through middle C and up onto the treble staff. Watch how the ledger line C connects the two worlds. This is the single most important sight-reading pattern in all of piano music.

Example 3 is a two-measure left-hand pattern in C major that you can loop with your right hand playing chords on top later. Once your eyes trust the low staff, patterns like this stop feeling like "another line to read" and start feeling like an accompaniment you already hear.

Exercises for the week

  1. Set a timer for 60 seconds. Point at any note on the bass staff, name it, move on. Aim for 30 correct names in a row without hesitation by Friday.
  2. Play a slow bass clef C major scale (C to C, one octave) with your left hand while saying each note name aloud.
  3. Take a piano piece you already like (a hymn, a pop ballad, anything with two hands) and read only the bass clef line, slowly. Ignore the treble entirely for 5 minutes.
  4. On guitar, play the open low E, A, D and say "bottom space, top space, middle line." Wire the picture and the sound together.
Ear-training rep (2 to 3 minutes) Sing the interval of a perfect fifth, low to high, using the first two notes of "Twinkle, Twinkle." Then find that same interval on the low strings of the guitar (open E to open B on the 5th string's 2nd fret) or on the piano (low C to G). Sing, then check. Sing, then check.
Try this week Pick one song you are working on or writing. Rewrite the melody's first phrase in bass clef (transpose it down an octave if you need to). Reading it in the new clef will lock the note names in faster than any flashcard drill.

Circle of Fifths

Two rules to keep glued to the front of your brain: clockwise adds sharps in the order F C G D A E B, and counterclockwise adds flats in the order B E A D G C F (the same letters, reversed).

This week's drill: Neighbors are your chords

Every key on the circle has two next-door neighbors, and those neighbors are secretly the two most important chords in that key. The neighbor one step clockwise is your V (dominant, the tension chord). The neighbor one step counterclockwise is your IV (subdominant, the "gospel" chord). Together with your I, they are almost every rock, country, and folk song ever written.

Try it: G is your I. Look at the wheel. One click clockwise from G is D (that's your V). One click counterclockwise from G is C (that's your IV). Boom, G-C-D. That's "Sweet Home Alabama," "Wagon Wheel," "Knockin' on Heaven's Door," and roughly a thousand others.

Key of the Day: G major Answer these before opening the drawer below.
  1. How many sharps does G major have, and which one?
  2. Name all seven notes of the G major scale in order.
  3. What is the relative minor of G major?
  4. What are the I, IV, and V chords in G major?
Show answers

1) One sharp, F sharp. 2) G, A, B, C, D, E, F#, (G). 3) E minor (three half steps below G, sharing the same key signature). 4) I = G, IV = C, V = D. Play them once through on piano and once on guitar, then jam a I-IV-V-I loop.

Vocal Routine (about 25 minutes)

This week's focus is vowel modification as you ascend, which is the single most useful tool a baritone has for softening the first passaggio around A3 to B-flat3. The idea: as you climb into the break, you slowly reshape the vowel so the resonance stays balanced instead of the tone splatting or flipping.

Part A: Warm-up (6 to 8 minutes)

Start with appoggio breathing: hand on the low ribs, inhale slowly for 4, hiss out for 8, four rounds. Then lip trills on a 5-note scale (do-re-mi-fa-sol-fa-mi-re-do), starting on C3 and moving up chromatically to about F3. Finish with three soft "mmm" sirens from the bottom of your comfortable range up through G4 and back down. Gentle only, no push.

Part B: Vowel modification ascending (about 15 minutes)

The rule of thumb: as you climb, brighter and more open vowels want to modify toward a rounder, taller shape. "Ee" leans toward "ih," "ay" leans toward "eh," "ah" leans toward "aw," and "oh" stays "oh" but with a taller inside of the mouth. You are not swallowing the word, you are letting the space inside grow with the pitch.

Drill 1: sing a 5-note scale (do-re-mi-fa-sol) on the vowel "ee," starting on F3 and going up by half step. Around A3 (start of your primo passaggio), consciously let the "ee" round toward "ih" on the top note only. Descend the same way. Move up by half step to G3, A-flat3, A3, B-flat3, B3, C4.

Drill 2: same 5-note scale on "ah." Around the passaggio, allow "ah" to open toward "aw," and let the back of the tongue drop slightly so the vowel sounds a little rounder and darker at the top. Do not push volume.

Drill 3: 1-3-5-3-1 arpeggio on "oh," starting on F3. As you approach the top note in each arpeggio starting from A3, imagine a tall interior space (a small yawn feeling behind the vowel). This is a great one for keeping the secondo passaggio (D4 to E-flat4) navigable when we get there in future weeks.

Safety Hydrate before and during. Keep volume moderate, especially through the passaggio. If anything hurts, tickles, or sounds hoarse, stop for the day. Modification is a subtle nudge, not a lurch, so if you feel the vowel changing suddenly, back the pitch down a step and try again with less shift.

Part C: Cool-down (2 to 3 minutes)

Descending lip trills from around G4 down to C3, then a few gentle sighs from a comfortable high note down to your speaking pitch. Rehydrate. Notice how your speaking voice feels: warm and easy is the goal, not tired.
